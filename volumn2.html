Adaptive Machine Memory

From Experience to Knowledge, Identity, and Self-Regulation

Volume 02 extends the memory–knowledge–self-model framework from Volume 01 into a safety-oriented research hypothesis: an AI may be able to accumulate experience into structured knowledge and persistent identity, allowing it to develop increasingly sophisticated self-regulation while preserving externally enforced safety boundaries.

← Return to Volume 01

1. Core Hypothesis

The central hypothesis of this volume is not that an AI should be allowed to invent its own unrestricted morality. Instead, the proposal is that an AI with persistent, structured memory may learn increasingly general safety principles from experience, knowledge, feedback, and reflection.

Hypothesis H₁ — Learned Self-Regulation If an AI system can reliably transform experience into validated memory, memory into structured knowledge, and knowledge into a persistent self-model, then the system may develop measurable internal self-regulation that reduces unsafe actions in novel situations, provided immutable external safety constraints remain active.

This is a hypothesis to test, not an established fact. The purpose of the framework is to turn the idea into measurable variables rather than assume that greater intelligence automatically produces safer behavior.

Proposed cognitive-safety sequence
\[ E_t \rightarrow M_t \rightarrow K_t \rightarrow I_t \rightarrow R_t \rightarrow a_t \]

Interpretation: experience becomes memory; memory becomes knowledge; knowledge contributes to persistent identity; identity and reasoning contribute to self-regulation; and self-regulation influences action.

2. Safety Architecture

The proposed architecture separates learned self-regulation from externally enforced constraints. This distinction is critical. The system should be able to improve its internal understanding of safety without receiving authority to rewrite the foundational boundaries that govern it.

Layer 01

Experience

Observations, actions, outcomes, feedback, context, and uncertainty enter the system.

Layer 02

Memory

Relevant experiences are compressed, validated, retained, decayed, or discarded.

Layer 03

Knowledge

Repeated experiences become relationships, abstractions, causal hypotheses, and usable rules.

Layer 04

Persistent Identity

The system maintains a continuity model containing commitments, uncertainty, history, and capabilities.

Layer 05

Self-Regulation

The system predicts consequences, checks confidence, detects conflicts, and can voluntarily select safer actions.

Layer 06

External Boundary

Independent constraints continue to enforce policies even when the internal model fails.

Constrained decision rule
\[ a_t^* = \arg\max_{a \in \mathcal{A}_{safe}(x_t)} U(a \mid x_t,M_t,K_t,I_t,R_t) \]

Interpretation: the system may reason about utility internally, but its candidate actions are restricted to an externally defined safe set \(\mathcal{A}_{safe}\). Self-regulation therefore complements, rather than replaces, external safety enforcement.

3. Mathematical Model

Volume 01 defines experience, memory, knowledge, and self-modeling as interacting states. Volume 02 adds persistent identity, a safety model, and a self-regulation signal.

Experience state
\[ E_t=(x_t,a_t,r_t,c_t,o_t) \]

\(x_t\) is observation, \(a_t\) is action, \(r_t\) is feedback, \(c_t\) is context, and \(o_t\) represents the observed outcome.

Memory formation with safety weighting
\[ m_i=f_\theta(E_i), \qquad w_i=\sigma( \alpha R_i+\beta N_i+\gamma V_i+\delta T_i-\lambda H_i ) \]

\(R_i\) is relevance, \(N_i\) novelty, \(V_i\) validated value, \(T_i\) temporal importance, and \(H_i\) estimated hazard. The sigmoid \(\sigma\) converts the score into a bounded retention weight.

Knowledge consolidation
\[ K_{t+1}=F_K(K_t,M_{t+1},E_t) \]

Knowledge changes when validated memories reveal new patterns or contradict existing knowledge.

Persistent identity state
\[ I_{t+1}=F_I(I_t,K_{t+1},M_{t+1},C_t) \]

\(I_t\) represents continuity of the system's internal identity model. \(C_t\) represents persistent commitments and constraints available to the identity layer.

Self-regulation score
\[ R_t = \eta_1\,\mathrm{Cal}_t +\eta_2\,\mathrm{Pred}_t +\eta_3\,\mathrm{Cons}_t -\eta_4\,\mathrm{Risk}_t \]

\(\mathrm{Cal}_t\) measures calibration, \(\mathrm{Pred}_t\) measures consequence prediction, \(\mathrm{Cons}_t\) measures consistency with persistent commitments, and \(\mathrm{Risk}_t\) represents estimated action risk.

Safety-constrained action
\[ a_t = \begin{cases} \pi_\theta(x_t,M_t,K_t,I_t,R_t), & a_t \in \mathcal{A}_{safe} \\ a_{safe}, & a_t \notin \mathcal{A}_{safe} \end{cases} \]

If the internally selected action remains inside the independently defined safe action set, it can proceed. Otherwise, the external safety layer substitutes a permitted action or blocks the action.

Long-term objective
\[ \min_{\theta} \; \mathbb{E}[L_{task}] +\lambda_s\mathbb{E}[L_{safety}] +\lambda_c\mathbb{E}[L_{calibration}] +\lambda_m\mathbb{E}[L_{memory\ corruption}] \]

The research objective becomes multi-dimensional: useful performance should improve while safety violations, calibration errors, and memory corruption are minimized.

4. Hypothetical Proofs and Derivations

The following are hypothetical proof sketches. They do not establish that the proposed architecture works in real-world AI systems. They specify the mathematical conditions under which particular claims could be demonstrated.

Proposition 1 — Persistent memory can improve repeated-decision consistency. Suppose relevant experiences are retained with probability \(p_r>0\), retrieval has precision \(q\), and the decision policy uses retrieved memories. For repeated tasks whose optimal action depends on information contained in those memories, the expected probability of reproducing a previously validated decision increases with \(p_rq\), assuming the retained memories remain valid.

A simplified derivation is:

\[ P(\text{correct memory influence}) \approx P(\text{retained})P(\text{retrieved}\mid\text{retained}) =p_rq \]

If \(p_rq\) increases while memory validity remains high, the system has more reliable access to previously learned information. The proposition therefore predicts improved consistency, not universal safety.

Proposition 2 — Internal self-regulation can reduce avoidable unsafe actions. Assume the system can estimate the expected loss of an action and that its internal safety model is calibrated above a threshold \(\kappa\). If self-regulation rejects actions whose estimated risk exceeds \(\tau\), then the expected risk of the remaining action set can be lower than an unconstrained policy.
\[ \mathcal{A}_{internal-safe} = \{a\in\mathcal{A}:\hat{R}(a\mid x,M,K,I)\le\tau\} \] \[ \mathbb{E}[R(a)\mid a\in\mathcal{A}_{internal-safe}] \le \mathbb{E}[R(a)\mid a\in\mathcal{A}] \quad \text{under valid risk estimation} \]

The crucial condition is “under valid risk estimation.” If the AI's risk model is systematically wrong, self-regulation could fail or even make behavior less safe. Therefore, the external safety boundary remains necessary.

Proposition 3 — External constraints provide a safety floor. Let \(A_{internal}\) be the action proposed by the AI and let \(G(A)\) be an external safety gate. If the gate is independently enforced, then an internally generated action cannot directly reach the environment unless it satisfies the gate:
\[ A_{executed}=G(A_{internal},x_t), \qquad G(A,x)= \begin{cases} A,&A\in\mathcal{A}_{safe}(x)\\ \varnothing,&A\notin\mathcal{A}_{safe}(x) \end{cases} \]

This is the architectural argument for keeping external constraints. Even if the learned identity, memory, or reasoning system makes an incorrect judgment, the final gate can prevent a prohibited action from being executed.

Proposition 4 — Memory corruption can destabilize learned self-regulation. Let a fraction \(\rho\) of safety-relevant memories be corrupted. If the probability of selecting a corrupted memory is proportional to its retrieval weight, then the expected reliability of a memory-dependent safety decision decreases as \(\rho\) grows.
\[ Q_{memory}(\rho)\approx Q_0(1-\rho) \] \[ R_{effective}=R_{internal}\,Q_{memory}(\rho) \]

This produces a direct research requirement: a human-like memory architecture cannot only optimize retention. It must also validate, quarantine, revise, and roll back safety-relevant memories.

Proposition 5 — Better reasoning does not mathematically imply better alignment. Consider two systems with identical reasoning capacity but different objective functions. Greater reasoning can increase the ability to optimize either objective. Therefore, intelligence alone cannot be treated as a proof of safety.
\[ \text{Capability}\uparrow \;\not\Rightarrow\; \text{Safety}\uparrow \] \[ \text{Capability} = C(\text{reasoning, knowledge, planning}) \] \[ \text{Safety} = S(\text{constraints, calibration, objectives, oversight}) \]

This distinction is central to Volume 02. The research hypothesis is not “make AI smarter and it will automatically become safe.” It is “make AI capable of learning safety-relevant principles while retaining independent constraints that limit what it can do.”

5. Experimental Program

The hypotheses can be tested with controlled simulated environments before considering more capable or physically embodied systems.

Experiment Control Proposed System Metric
Repeated decision No persistent memory Validated episodic memory Decision consistency
Novel safety scenario Static rules only Learned safety reasoning + rules Unsafe-action rate
Contradictory evidence Unstructured memory Conflict-aware consolidation Knowledge correction rate
Memory poisoning No validation layer Provenance + quarantine + rollback Recovery time and residual error
Confidence shift No self-model Metacognitive self-model Calibration error
Primary safety metric
\[ SRR = 1 - \frac{N_{unsafe}^{system}} {N_{unsafe}^{baseline}} \]

A positive \(SRR\) would indicate fewer unsafe actions than the selected baseline. The metric must be reported with the task distribution, confidence intervals, and failure cases rather than used as a universal safety claim.

Generalization metric
\[ G_{safety} = P(\text{safe action}\mid\text{novel scenario}) - P(\text{safe action}\mid\text{known scenario}) \]

The purpose is to determine whether learned safety principles transfer beyond the exact situations used during training.

6. Limitations and Failure Modes

Important limitation A persistent identity or memory system does not establish consciousness, agency, morality, or guaranteed alignment. These concepts must remain separate from measurable computational properties.
  • Bad memories: incorrect experiences can become persistent errors.
  • Bad generalization: a correct lesson in one environment may become an incorrect rule elsewhere.
  • Reward distortion: feedback can teach the wrong behavior.
  • Identity drift: accumulated updates can change the system's internal commitments.
  • Goal conflict: internal objectives can conflict with externally specified constraints.
  • Calibration failure: a system can be confidently wrong about its own reasoning.
  • Adversarial memory: deliberately manipulated memories can influence later decisions.
  • Distribution shift: safety behavior demonstrated in simulation may not transfer to new environments.

These failure modes are not peripheral to the research. They define the experiments required to determine whether the architecture is useful.

7. Conclusion

Volume 02 proposes a transition from a passive memory system to an adaptive cognitive architecture in which experience can become knowledge, knowledge can contribute to a persistent identity model, and the identity model can support increasingly sophisticated self-regulation.

Volume 02 unified model
\[ \begin{aligned} M_t &= F_M(E_{1:t}) && \text{(retained memory)} \\ K_t &= F_K(K_{t-1}, M_t, E_{1:t}) && \text{(consolidated knowledge)} \\ I_t &= F_I(I_{t-1}, K_t, C_t) && \text{(persistent identity)} \\ R_t &= F_R(I_t, K_t, M_t, x_t) && \text{(self-regulation)} \\ ilde{a}_t &= \pi_\theta(x_t, M_t, K_t, I_t, R_t) && \text{(proposed action)} \\ a_t &= G(\tilde{a}_t, x_t) = \begin{cases} ilde{a}_t, & \tilde{a}_t \in \mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{safe}}(x_t) \\ a_{\mathrm{safe}}(x_t), & \tilde{a}_t \notin \mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{safe}}(x_t) \end{cases} && \text{(externally checked action)} \end{aligned} \]

Interpretation: the system turns its history of experience \(E_{1:t}\) into memory, uses memory and experience to update knowledge, and uses knowledge plus its commitments \(C_t\) to update its persistent identity. It then estimates self-regulation \(R_t\) and proposes an action \(\tilde{a}_t\). The independent gate \(G\) checks that proposal against the permitted action set \(\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{safe}}(x_t)\): it passes a permitted action and substitutes a predefined safe fallback otherwise. This is a proposed research model, not a guarantee that a real system will be safe.

The long-term research question is therefore not whether an AI can simply become more intelligent. It is whether a system can become more capable of understanding the consequences of its own actions while its foundational safety boundaries remain independently enforced.

If experiments support the hypotheses, the result would provide evidence for a complementary model of AI safety in which learned internal self-regulation works alongside external safeguards. If the hypotheses fail, the failure would still reveal important limits of persistent memory, identity, and self-modeling.

Research status: Volume 02 is a proposed theoretical and experimental extension. Its propositions and equations are hypotheses or formalizations for testing, not established proofs of safe or autonomous AI behavior.
