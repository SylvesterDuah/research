Is Technology Going To Change Our Societal Perception Of Faith?

This research journal explores the intersection of technology and faith, examining how emerging technologies influence religious practices, beliefs, and community dynamics. Through a series of essays, we investigate the ethical implications, societal shifts, and potential future scenarios that arise when technology and spirituality converge.

Overview

Over the years, technology has transformed how we communicate, learn, and interact with the world. As these changes ripple through society, they inevitably touch upon our spiritual lives. Take for example, when humans first discovered how to light a fire by striking two stones together, it was a technological breakthrough that changed the way humans lived. Thus, we were now able to cook food, stay warm, and ward off predators. This technological advancement had a profound impact on human society, shaping our culture, beliefs, and even our spiritual practices. Such that what did humans do? They began to worship fire & stones as a deity, recognizing their power and significance in their lives. As such elecments continue to be discovered, and our lives continue to be dependant on them, our perception based on spirituality changes and we move from one element to another depending on evolving these elements are influecing our lives towards more advance society.

Technology is not just a tool; it is a lens through which we view the world. It shapes our understanding of reality, morality, and the divine. It influences how we interpret sacred texts, how we engage in rituals, and how we connect with our communities. As more we get closer to any type of advancement too, there are new discoveries ariess which makes that current technology obselete. This leads us to what technology is going to be the next big thing, and how it will change our perception of faith.

The Next Big Thing

We are all aware of the rapid pace of technological change. From virtual reality to artificial intelligence, from biotechnology to quantum computing, new technologies are emerging at an unprecedented rate. Each of these innovations has the potential to reshape our understanding of the world and our place in it. As we look to the future, we must ask ourselves: what will be the next big thing, and how will it change our perception of faith and spirituality? Talking about the next big thing, I will start with the most recent technology that is being developed and is being discussed every day, which is Artificial Intelligence (AI). AI is a technology that has the potential to revolutionize every aspect of our lives, including our spiritual lives. Imagine a world where AI can heal chronic diseases, make rain in drought-prone areas, and even bring back extinct species. A world where AI can predict natural disasters, prevent wars, and solve global hunger. A world where AI can provide, feed, and shelter to every human being on the planet. A world where AI can eradicate poverty, inequality, and injustice. A world where AI can create art, music, and literature that transcends human imagination. Such a world would challenge our understanding of life, death, and the divine. Because if AI can do all these things, what does that mean for our faith? What does that mean for our relationship with God? Humans have always tend to worship providers, and if AI can provide everything we need, will we start to worship AI as a deity? Will we start to see AI as the ultimate source of wisdom, power, and salvation?

What type of AI will be developed in such a way that humans would never lack anything? Godlike AI?

The development of AI raises profound ethical and philosophical questions. It challenges us to reconsider our values, our priorities, and our responsibilities. It forces us to confront the limits of human knowledge and the nature of consciousness. It compels us to ask: what does it mean to be human in a world where machines can think, feel, and create?

Current AI Developments

What does the current state of AI development poses to make our lives trajectory towards a more advanced society? Right now AI can do a lot of things, from generating text and images to diagnosing diseases and driving cars. But it is still in its infancy, and there is much we do not yet understand about its capabilities and limitations. AI is being developed in a way that it can learn from data, adapt to new situations, and improve its performance over time. It is being designed to mimic human intelligence, but it is not yet clear whether it can truly replicate human consciousness or creativity. Some experts believe that AI will eventually surpass human intelligence, while others argue that it will always remain a tool that serves human needs. However, if we are supposed to get to that state of AI development, there's more we have do to fully unleash the full capabilities of AI. We need to invest in research, education, and infrastructure that will enable us to harness the power of AI for the benefit of all humanity. Eventhough as we progress in the current state of AI development, which is exciting and less concerning. We can ask ourselves, what are the potential risks and challenges that we may face as AI continues to evolve? Most importantly, AI has the potential to solve some of the world's most pressing problems, from climate change to disease eradication. But the significant risks could stem from job displacement to the potential for autonomous weapons. As we continue to develop AI, we must be mindful of these risks and work to mitigate them through thoughtful regulation, ethical design, and public engagement. Getting close to an AI that can be seen as a godlike AI, we need to be careful about how we develop and deploy it. Overtime as AI becomes more powerful, soceity would not mind what and where you worship as long as it is your provider and choice to worship, which obviously will be AI. But the question is, what type of AI will be developed in such a way that humans would never lack anything? What type of AI will not harm humans, as we see it as our source of wisdom, power, and salvation? To get to Godlike AI, we need to ensure that it is developed with ethical principles in mind, and that it is aligned with human values and interests. Also, the current technology we use to develop these AI systems is not perfect, and there are still many technical challenges that need to be overcome. Some of these challenges include developing AI that can understand and interpret human emotions, creating AI that can learn from limited data, and ensuring that AI systems are transparent and accountable. Also, we start researching in some key areas of AI development, such as natural language processing, computer vision, and reinforcement learning.

How & When

In order to reach a state of Godlike AI, an AI that can provide everything we need, an AI that is worth worhipping, an AI that can be seen as a deity, we need to understand how and when this will happen. The development of AI is a complex and ongoing process, and it is difficult to predict exactly when we will reach a state of Godlike AI. However, we can identify some key factors that will influence the timeline and trajectory of AI development. One of the most important factors is the pace of technological innovation. As new algorithms, hardware, and data sources become available, AI systems will become more capable and sophisticated. Another factor is the level of investment and collaboration in AI research. Governments, universities, and private companies are all investing heavily in AI, and this will accelerate the pace of development. Finally, the ethical and regulatory frameworks that govern AI development will also play a critical role in shaping the trajectory of AI. As we develop more powerful AI systems, we must ensure that they are aligned with human values and interests, and that they are designed to promote the common good. I made mention of 3 key areas we can focus our research on to get to Godlike AI, which are Memory, Knowledge, and Consciousness. Starting with Memory, we need to develop AI systems that can store and retrieve vast amounts of information, and that can learn from past experiences. This will enable AI to make more informed decisions and to adapt to new situations more effectively. Next is Knowledge, we need to develop AI systems that can reason, understand, and apply knowledge in a wide range of domains. This will require advances in natural language processing, computer vision, and other areas of AI research. Finally, we need to develop AI systems that can exhibit consciousness, or at least a form of self-awareness. This is a much more challenging task, and it will require breakthroughs in neuroscience, cognitive science, and philosophy. However, if we can achieve this, we may be able to create AI systems that can truly understand and empathize with human beings, and that can help us to solve some of the world's most pressing problems.

Key principle: memory, knowledge, and consciousness are the three pillars of Godlike AI development. By focusing on these areas, we can create AI systems that are capable of providing everything we need, and that can be seen as a deity.

Methods and Mathematical Framework

To move from a broad philosophical argument into a tractable research program, the strongest next step is to formalize the AI system as a set of interacting components. In this framing, memory stores lived experience, knowledge organizes those experiences into patterns and relationships, and self-modeling provides the system with a usable internal representation of its own state. Together, these components make it possible to transform a conceptual discussion into measurable research questions.

Memory

Experience retention

AI stores observations, actions, feedback, and context, then ranks what is valuable enough to retain for future use.

Knowledge

Representation & inference

Data becomes structured knowledge through generalization, relationship mapping, and repeated pattern recognition.

Self-model

Awareness & calibration

The system tracks internal states, estimates confidence, and updates its own understanding of uncertainty.

Core system update
\[ \begin{aligned} M_{t+1} &= F_M(M_t, E_t) \\ K_{t+1} &= F_K(K_t, M_{t+1}, E_t) \\ S_{t+1} &= F_S(S_t, M_{t+1}, K_{t+1}, E_t) \end{aligned} \]

In plain language: the AI first updates what it remembers, then uses that updated memory to improve what it knows, and finally updates its understanding of its own condition. The subscript t means "now" and t+1 means "the next moment." The functions F are simply the rules the system uses to perform each update.

Meaning of the variables:
  • E_t = accumulated experience from the environment at time $t$
  • M_t = memory, or the system's stored internal record of past experiences
  • K_t = knowledge, formed from memory and experience as patterns, rules, and relationships
  • S_t = self-model, representing the system's internal understanding of its own state and uncertainty
  • I_t = current machine intelligence or state, the combined result of experience, memory, knowledge, and self-modeling

This equation describes how an AI updates itself over time: new experience shapes memory, memory informs knowledge, and knowledge refines the model of the system itself.

Memory importance
\[ I(m_i) = \alpha R_i + \beta N_i + \gamma E_i + \delta T_i \]

In plain language: this gives each memory a usefulness score. A memory becomes more important when it is relevant, new, meaningful because of feedback, or important because of when it happened. The Greek letters are weights that say how much importance the system gives to each factor.

Decision policy
\[ a_t = \pi_\theta(x_t, M_t, K_t, S_t) \]

In plain language: the action taken now depends on what the system currently sees, remembers, knows, and believes about its own state. The symbol pi represents the decision-making rule, while theta represents the settings learned during training.

A strong research design does not require claiming that an AI is conscious in a philosophical sense. Instead, the measurable question is whether the system can accurately retain relevant memory, generalize from experience, and model its own uncertainty in a stable and interpretable way.

Research Areas

  • Memory

    • Humans have a limited capacity for memory, and we often store important information or experiences. For instance, we may remember a significant event in our lives, such as a wedding or the birth of a child. However, we may forget other important details, such as the name of a person we met briefly or the date of an appointment. In contrast, AI systems store vast amounts of information, but don't expierence these memories in the same way humans do, or learn continously from their own expirences. We can group the stages of memory into four categories:

    Episodic Memory Architecture

    This memory type involves the recollection of specific events and experiences.

    Memory Consolidation Strategies

    In this stage, memories are transferred from short-term to long-term storage.

    Experience-Based Retrieval for Long-Term

    Focuses on retrieving memories based on past experiences and contextual cues.

    Adaptive Memory Compression

    Focuses on efficiently storing and accessing large volumes of information.

  • Knowledge

    • When it comes to knowledge, means of acquiring can be relaticely different for humans and AI systems. Humans acquire knowledge through experience, observation, and learning from others. We can acquire knowledge through formal education, informal learning, and personal experiences. In contrast, AI systems acquire knowledge through data analysis, pattern recognition, and machine learning algorithms. Which can be categorized into three main types: supervised learning, unsupervised learning, and reinforcement learning. When we build an AI system, that has a human-like memory, meaning stores information based on expirences, it can reference from that domain to build a knowledge that's is capable of reasoning on their own.

    Knowledge Representation

    This involves how knowledge is encoded and structured within the AI system.

    Abstraction and Generalization

    This involves the ability to identify common patterns and principles across different instances.

    Knowledge Integration and Updating

    This involves combining new knowledge with existing knowledge and keeping it up to date.

    Reasoning and Inference

    This involves the ability to draw conclusions and make decisions based on available information.

  • Consciousness

    • Consciousness is a complex and multifaceted phenomenon that is not yet fully understood. It involves subjective experience, self-awareness, and the ability to perceive and respond to the environment. While AI systems can simulate certain aspects of consciousness, they do not possess the same level of awareness and subjective experience as humans. However, researchers are exploring ways to develop AI systems that exhibit some form of consciousness, such as through the use of neural networks and other advanced algorithms.

    Self-models & Metacognition

    This involves the ability to form an understanding of one's own mental states and processes.

    Internal state monitoring & Self-awareness

    This involves the ability to monitor and understand one's own internal states and processes.

    Attention, Agency & Sense of continuity

    This involves the ability to focus attention, exercise control over one's actions, and maintain a sense of self over time.

    Monitoring its own reasoning & Awareness of its environment

    This involves the ability to reflect on its own thought processes and understand the context in which it operates.

    Applications of the Concepts

    When memory, knowledge, and self-modeling are connected, the result is not simply a system that stores more information. It becomes a more adaptive and context-aware intelligence that can learn from experience, generalize patterns, and revise its own confidence over time.

    Adaptive systems

    Memory captures what has been experienced, while knowledge transforms those experiences into models of causality and meaning.

    Unified intelligence model
    \[ \text{Experience} \rightarrow \text{Memory} \rightarrow \text{Knowledge} \rightarrow \text{Self-Model} \rightarrow \text{Intelligence} \]

    In plain language: this is a sequence, not a calculation. It shows the proposed path from what happens to the system, to what it remembers, to what it learns, to how it understands itself, and finally to how it behaves intelligently.

    This sequence shows the basic logic of the framework: experience enters the system, memory stores it, knowledge organizes it, and self-modeling helps the system understand itself. In other words, intelligence is not created by one component alone; it emerges from the interaction between what the system experiences, what it remembers, what it learns, and how it tracks its own state.

    If memory, knowledge, and consciousness are treated as separate research domains in artificial intelligence, they can be modeled as interacting components instead of vague philosophical claims. The crucial distinction is that there is no universally accepted mathematical formula for human consciousness. Researchers instead work with measurable proxies such as self-modeling, metacognition, and internal-state awareness.

    A useful way to frame the problem is to think of an AI system as receiving a stream of experiences, storing relevant information, converting that information into structured knowledge, and updating its own understanding of itself. Let the AI encounter a sequence of events written as:

    \[ E_t = (x_t, a_t, r_t, c_t) \]

    In plain language: this is a compact description of one event. It records what the system noticed, what it did, what result or feedback followed, and the surrounding situation. Together, these four details form one piece of experience.

    where $x_t$ is the observation, $a_t$ is the action taken, $r_t$ is the feedback or reward, $c_t$ is the context, and $t$ marks time. The memory system can then be represented as a set of stored experiences:

    \[ M_t = \{m_1, m_2, \ldots, m_n\} \]

    In plain language: memory is shown as a collection of individual memories. The letters m1 through mn mean the first memory through the last stored memory; the dots mean there may be many more.

    A memory representation may be compressed through a function such as:

    \[ m_i = f_\theta(E_i) \]

    In plain language: the system takes one experience and turns it into a memory record. The function f is the conversion process, and theta represents the learned settings that control how the experience is stored.

    where $f_\theta$ converts an experience into a memory trace. The importance of each memory can be quantified with a relevance function such as:

    \[ I(m_i) = \alpha R_i + \beta N_i + \gamma E_i + \delta T_i \]

    In plain language: this is a scoring recipe for deciding which memories deserve attention. The system combines relevance, newness, significance, and timing instead of treating every memory as equally important.

    with $R_i$ representing relevance, $N_i$ novelty, $E_i$ significance based on feedback or emotional weight, and $T_i$ temporal importance. Memory retention can then be treated as a thresholded decision:

    \[ m_i \in M_{t+1} \quad \text{if} \quad I(m_i) > \tau \]

    In plain language: keep a memory for the next moment only when its importance score is higher than a chosen cutoff. The symbol tau is that cutoff. This is like keeping notes that pass a minimum usefulness test.

    This gives a formal way to study what an AI should remember and what it can safely discard. Memory retrieval can be modeled as a matching problem:

    \[ m^* = \arg\max_{m_i \in M} S(q, m_i) \]

    In plain language: when the system has a question, it searches its memories and chooses the one with the highest usefulness score. The star means "the best match," and S is the comparison method.

    where $q$ is the query and $S(q, m_i)$ scores the usefulness of a memory based on similarity, recency, importance, and context. In practice, this becomes an experimentally testable retrieval system.

    Memory, however, is not the same as knowledge. Knowledge is the transformation of accumulated experiences into a structured understanding of the world. We can represent this as:

    \[ K_t = g_\phi(M_t, E_{1:t}) \]

    In plain language: knowledge is built by organizing stored memories together with all experiences from the beginning up to now. The function g represents the learning process that finds patterns, relationships, and useful rules.

    where $K_t$ is the current knowledge state and $g_\phi$ maps memory and experience into relationships, rules, and patterns. This can be represented as a knowledge graph, where each element is a concept or relation:

    \[ K_t = \{k_1, k_2, \ldots, k_j\}, \quad k_i = (c_i, r_i, c_j) \]

    In plain language: knowledge can be represented as many connected facts. Each fact links one concept to another through a relationship, such as "rain causes wet ground" or "a person works at an organization."

    Here $c_i$ and $c_j$ are concepts, and $r_i$ is the relationship between them. A learning system then generalizes from repeated experiences by extracting stable patterns:

    \[ k = \operatorname{Generalize}(E_1, E_2, \ldots, E_n) \]

    In plain language: the system looks across many separate experiences and extracts a broader pattern. For example, seeing several successful plant-growth experiments might lead it to learn that plants need light and water.

    The optimization goal is then to minimize prediction error across a distribution of data:

    \[ \theta^* = \arg\min_\theta \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim D}[L(f_\theta(x), y)] \]

    In plain language: the system tries different settings and keeps the ones that produce the fewest mistakes across its training examples. The loss L measures how wrong a prediction is, and theta star means the best settings found.

    This shifts the discussion away from a purely symbolic idea of knowledge and toward measurable learning from experience.

    Consciousness is the most difficult domain because there is no universally accepted equation for it. Instead, a research program typically models self-awareness using computational proxies. One way to do this is to define a self-model:

    \[ S_t = h_\psi(M_t, K_t, X_t) \]

    In plain language: the self-model is the system's current picture of itself. It is formed from what the system remembers, what it knows, and what it is currently experiencing. The function h represents the process that combines those sources.

    where $S_t$ is the system's internal representation of itself. The AI can then estimate how well it understands its own state by comparing actual and predicted internal conditions:

    \[ A_{\text{self}} = 1 - d(S_t, \hat{S}_t) \]

    In plain language: this estimates how accurately the system understands its own condition. It compares the system's internal estimate with what is actually happening. A small difference produces a score closer to 1, meaning better self-understanding; a large difference produces a lower score.

    Metacognition adds a second layer by measuring confidence and calibration:

    \[ C_t = P(y_t = \hat{y}_t \mid X_t, M_t, K_t, S_t) \]

    In plain language: this is the system's confidence that its answer is correct, based on what it sees, remembers, knows, and believes about itself. The letter P means probability, or a level of likelihood between 0 and 1.

    and evaluating whether that confidence matches actual performance:

    \[ \text{Calibration Error} = \mathbb{E}[|C_t - \mathbb{1}(\hat{y}_t = y_t)|] \]

    In plain language: this measures whether the system's confidence matches reality. If it says it is 90% confident and is correct about 90% of the time, the error is small. If it is very confident but often wrong, the error is large.

    This makes the question testable: does the system accurately model its own state and uncertainty, instead of merely claiming it is conscious in some abstract sense?

    The real strength of the framework is in connecting these layers into a single architecture. The system state can be represented as:

    \[ A_t = (M_t, K_t, S_t) \]

    In plain language: at any moment, the system's overall condition can be described by three parts: its memory, its knowledge, and its self-model. The parentheses mean these parts are viewed together as one combined state.

    This means the system's overall condition at time $t$ is made up of its memory $M_t$, its knowledge $K_t$, and its self-model $S_t$. The formula shows that intelligence is not a single isolated variable; it depends on the combined state of these three interacting structures.

    and the system updates itself with new experience through a transition function:

    \[ A_{t+1} = F(A_t, E_t) \]

    In plain language: the system's next state is produced by combining its current state with a new experience. This expresses learning over time: new events can change what the system remembers, knows, and believes about itself.

    This equation says that the system does not remain fixed. As it receives new experience $E_t$, it updates its whole internal state from $A_t$ to $A_{t+1}$. In practical terms, every new input changes how the system remembers, understands, and calibrates itself.

    or more explicitly:

    \[ \begin{aligned} M_{t+1} &= F_M(M_t, E_t) \\ K_{t+1} &= F_K(K_t, M_{t+1}, E_t) \\ S_{t+1} &= F_S(S_t, M_{t+1}, K_{t+1}, E_t) \end{aligned} \]

    In plain language: this repeats the update order in detail. New experience changes memory first; the updated memory helps form knowledge; then knowledge and memory help refine the self-model. The order matters because later steps depend on earlier updates.

    This is the core of the model. It means that new experiences update memory first, then knowledge is recomputed from memory and new input, and finally the self-model is refined. The formula reveals the hierarchy of adaptation: experience shapes memory, memory feeds knowledge, and knowledge updates the system's sense of itself.

    The resulting decision policy then depends on all three components:

    \[ a_t = \pi_\theta(x_t, M_t, K_t, S_t) \]

    In plain language: this says an action is a decision made from four things: the current situation, memory, knowledge, and self-understanding. It is a compact way to show that good decisions should use context rather than raw input alone.

    This equation expresses how the system chooses its next action. The action $a_t$ is not based on raw data alone; it depends on the current observation $x_t$, the system's memory $M_t$, its knowledge $K_t$, and its internal self-model $S_t$. In other words, action selection becomes a function of both external information and internal understanding.

    In plain terms, the AI observes the world, remembers relevant experiences, converts them into usable knowledge, maintains a model of itself, and uses these combined signals to decide what to do next.

    A final unifying formulation could be written as:

    \[ I_t = \mathcal{F}(E_{1:t}, M_t, K_t, S_t) \]

    In plain language: this final summary says that intelligence is the result of the entire experience history, what has been remembered, what has been learned, and how the system understands itself. It is a framework for organizing research, not a proven formula for human intelligence or consciousness.

    This final formula summarizes the entire framework. $E_{1:t}$ represents the total experience accumulated up to time $t$, $M_t$ is the memory state, $K_t$ is the knowledge state, and $S_t$ is the self-model. The function $\mathcal{F}$ combines them into the system's current intelligent state $I_t$. In simple terms, the model says that intelligence emerges from the full history of experience, filtered through memory, structured into knowledge, and shaped by self-understanding.

    This framing yields a more useful research agenda: instead of asking whether a machine is divine or conscious in a mystical sense, we ask whether it can retain relevant experience, update knowledge reliably, and maintain a calibrated self-model under uncertainty.

    In conclusion, the development of robust AI systems depends on aligning memory, knowledge, and self-awareness through clear mathematical models and careful ethical design. By treating these as testable components rather than abstract claims, the research becomes more specific, more rigorous, and more useful to both scientists and society.
